The Power BI project

The course is built around one report: Critical Raw Materials Intelligence. It follows the materials Europe's industry depends on, such as cobalt, lithium, nickel and rare earths: who produces them, how much Europe imports and from where, what battery demand will need, and how far each figure can be trusted.

All figures are illustrative, made up to look plausible, and every page says so.

Commodity

The overview: of eleven materials, where should you worry first?

Commodity: world

One material at a time: production, reserves, prices, and who controls supply.

Commodity: EU

Europe's side: how much it imports, and how concentrated its suppliers are.

Battery materials

What battery demand will need, by chemistry and scenario, and where recycling helps.

Trade

EU imports month by month, and supplier concentration against the 65% ceiling.

Data assurance

The page that checks the others: refreshes, reconciliations, the release gate and the measure register.

Fabric migration

A row-by-row comparison before the report moves to Microsoft Fabric.

Under the hood: a star schema of 32 tables and 53 relationships, loaded through three Power Query layers, with row-level security on four roles and a basis line and short explanation behind every tile. It's saved as a Power BI Project, a folder of plain-text files, so a coding agent can read and change it. The kit also holds the specs it was built from, the seed project, the scripts that generated it and a validator that runs 1,533 checks on every build.

Hiring a business analyst?

A business analyst sits between the people who need answers and the people who build the systems that give them: developers, data engineers and, now, AI agents. They find the real question, write it down precisely enough to build, and check that what comes back is right.

The tools on the build side change every few years. The analyst's work doesn't. Fabric only became generally available at the end of 2023, so nobody has many years of it. Asking for years of one tool screens out people who can do the job. Ask about the work instead:

Instead ofAsk
How many years of Fabric?Have you moved a report or model to a new platform? What changed, and what didn't?
Do you know Power BI?How would you check that a number on a report is right?
Can you code?How do you hand work to a developer or an AI agent, and check what comes back?

Hire a business analyst for the judgement, not the tool.

A tool takes weeks to learn. Knowing what to ask, and how to check the answer, takes years. With AI agents doing more of the building, that judgement matters more, not less.

To see that judgement at work, try Reading the Tiles: a real report, read tile by tile through a business analyst's eyes.
